Part 3: The Unification of Humankind¶

Part 3 traces how thousands of isolated tribes and mini-worlds gradually merged into a single global civilisation, driven by three universal orders: money, empires, and religions.

Chapter 9: The Arrow of History¶

The Big Idea: Culture is never static; its internal contradictions drive constant change, and over millennia the clear long-term direction of history has been from thousands of tiny, isolated worlds towards one single global village.

Core Concepts & Notes¶

  • Cognitive Dissonance Is Culture's Engine: Every human culture is packed with built-in contradictions (e.g., medieval knights trying to reconcile Christian humility with chivalric pride; modern democracies juggling liberty with equality). Rather than being a bug, cognitive dissonance forces people to think, debate, and evolve their culture.
  • The Spy Satellite View of History: Zoom in on a single decade and history looks chaotic (empires rise and shatter). Zoom out to millennia and the trend is unmistakable:
    • 10,000 BCE: Thousands of completely separate human worlds.
    • 2000 BCE: Hundreds of worlds.
    • 1450 CE: Four major worlds (Afro-Asia, Mesoamerica, the Andes, Australia/Oceania), with 90% of humans already in Afro-Asia.
    • Today: One single global system (same science, same economics, same nation-state model, same sports).
  • The Myth of "Authentic" Cultures: What we celebrate today as "traditional" local cuisine or culture is almost always a recent mash-up of global imperial exchange.
  • The Three Universal Unifiers: For most of history, humans split the world into "Us" (the tribe) and "Them" (outsiders). Only in the last 3,000 years did three ideas emerge that claimed to apply to all humans everywhere:
    1. Economic: Money.
    2. Political: Empires.
    3. Religious: Universal religions (Buddhism, Christianity, Islam).

Mind-Blowing Anecdotes & Stories¶

  • The "Authentic" National Dish Illusion: Imagine a traditional Italian meal (tomato sauce on pasta), a Polish peasant dish (potatoes), an Argentine steakhouse (beef), an Indian curry (hot chillies), and Swiss chocolate. Before 1492, none of those existed in those places:
    • Tomatoes, chillies, potatoes, and cocoa came from the Americas (brought to Europe and India by Spanish and Portuguese empires).
    • Cattle and horses came from Eurasia (there wasn't a single cow in Argentina before Columbus).
    • In an ancient Roman pizzeria or a 14th-century Indian kitchen, there were no tomatoes and no chillies!

Memorable Quotes¶

"Consistency is the playground of dull minds."

"If people were unable to hold contradictory beliefs and values, it would probably be impossible to establish and maintain any human culture."

Chapter 10: The Scent of Money¶

The Big Idea: Money is the most universal and most efficient system of mutual trust ever devised; it is the only human myth that bridges every cultural, religious, and political divide.

Core Concepts & Notes¶

  • Why Barter Fails at Scale: If a shoemaker wants apples from an orchardist who doesn't need shoes, barter breaks down fast. In an economy with 100 goods, you'd need to memorise 4,950 exchange rates. Money solves this by providing a universal converter and a portable store of value.
  • Money Is Not Material, It Is Psychological: Coins, cowry shells, cigarettes in POW camps, and digital numbers in a bank database have almost zero intrinsic value. Today, more than 90% of all money exists only on computer servers (less than 10% is physical coins and banknotes).
  • Why Do People Trust Pieces of Paper? Because you trust that everyone else trusts them. Even bitter enemies who disagree on God, politics, and morality agree on the value of a gold coin or a US dollar.
  • How Money Unified the Globe: Even before European empires, gold and silver linked distant lands. If gold was cheaper in the Mediterranean than in India, merchants shipped gold east and spices west until prices equalised, tying distant civilisations into one economic web.
  • The Dark Side of Money: Money relies on trust, yet it can corrode community trust by turning priceless things (honour, loyalty, family care, justice) into tradable commodities. Humans constantly erect cultural dams (laws against selling children, votes, or organs) to stop the market from swallowing everything.

Mind-Blowing Anecdotes & Stories¶

  • Cortés and the Aztecs' "Disease of the Heart": When Hernán Cortés invaded Mexico in 1519, the Aztecs used cocoa beans and cloth as currency and saw gold mainly as a pretty, soft metal for jewellery. They were baffled by the Spaniards' manic obsession with gold. Cortés famously told them: "I and my companions suffer from a disease of the heart which can be cured only with gold."
  • Osama bin Laden and the US Dollar: Osama bin Laden hated American culture, American religion, and American politics with a passion, yet he had zero objection to American dollars. Money is the apogee of human tolerance: it doesn't ask for your passport, your god, or your ideology.
  • Roman Coins in India & Muslim Coins in Christian Europe: First-century Indians trusted Roman denarii stamped with the face of Emperor Tiberius so much that local Indian rulers minted their own imitations of Roman coins. Centuries later, devout Christian kings in medieval Europe minted gold coins inscribed in Arabic with the Islamic declaration of faith ("There is no god except Allah, and Muhammad is His messenger") simply because merchants trusted Muslim dinars.

Memorable Quotes¶

"Money is the most universal and most efficient system of mutual trust ever devised."

"Even people who do not believe in the same god or obey the same king are more than willing to use the same money."

"While money builds universal trust between strangers, this trust is invested not in humans, communities or sacred values, but in money itself and in the impersonal systems that back it."

Chapter 11: Imperial Visions¶

The Big Idea: Empires were built on blood and conquest, yet they have been the single most common form of political organisation for 2,500 years, and almost every culture we cherish today is the legacy of past empires.

Core Concepts & Notes¶

  • What Makes an Empire? Only two criteria: (1) ruling over a significant number of distinct peoples with different cultural identities and territories, and (2) having flexible borders and a potentially unlimited appetite to absorb more. (Size and form of government don't matter: democratic Athens and Britain were empires, as were autocratic Rome and Persia.)
  • The "Evil Empire" Myth: Today "imperialist" is one of the worst political insults, right next to "fascist". Yet empires were remarkably stable, often suppressed endless local tribal wars, and funded roads, law codes, philosophy, and art (from the Taj Mahal to Roman aqueducts).
  • From "Us vs. Them" to "All of Us" (The Imperial Mission): Starting with Cyrus the Great of Persia (c. 550 BCE), empires began justifying conquest not as mere plunder, but as a noble mission to bring peace, justice, and civilisation to "barbarians" (the Persian King of Kings, the Chinese Mandate of Heaven, the Roman Pax Romana, the British White Man's Burden).
  • Empires Are Cultural Blenders: Conquerors absorb the conquered, and over generations the conquered adopt the imperial language, law, and identity, eventually claiming it as their own. There is no "pure" pre-imperial culture left to return to.
  • The Emerging Global Empire: Today's independent nation-states are slowly losing real sovereignty to a de facto global empire of international law, global markets, multinational NGOs, and shared problems (climate change, nuclear weapons, AI) that no single country can solve alone.

Mind-Blowing Anecdotes & Stories¶

  • The Numantia Paradox (Rome and Spain): In 134 BCE, the Celtiberian city of Numantia resisted Roman conquest so fiercely that its citizens burned their own city and committed mass suicide rather than surrender to Scipio Aemilianus. Today, Spaniards celebrate Numantia as a symbol of Spanish heroism, yet they do so in Spanish (a dialect of Roman Latin), live under Roman law, worship a Roman-spread religion (Christianity), and call their hero Cervantes a Spaniard, not a Celtiberian. The conquered became Romans.
  • India's "Foreign" Inheritance: Modern independent India fiercely rejected British colonialism, yet it kept the British-built railway network, parliamentary democracy, English as a unifying national language, common law, tea, and cricket. Likewise, before the British, India's greatest architectural icon (the Taj Mahal) was built by the Mughals (a Turko-Mongol Muslim empire from Central Asia). If you stripped away every imperial legacy from India, what would remain?

Memorable Quotes¶

"It is simply impossible to untangle the imperial legacy from the local heritage."

"No matter how you look at it, the Taj Mahal is authentic Indian culture, even though it was built by Muslim conquerors from Central Asia."

Chapter 12: The Law of Religion¶

The Big Idea: Religion is a system of human norms and values founded on a belief in a superhuman order; it is the third great unifier of humankind, and modern secular ideologies like liberalism, communism, and nationalism function as religions too.

Core Concepts & Notes¶

  • Harari's Definition of Religion: Not necessarily belief in a God, but any system of human norms grounded in a superhuman order (something humans did not invent and cannot vote to change). To be a global unifier, a religion must be both universal (true everywhere) and missionary (must be spread to everyone).
  • From Animism to Polytheism: When humans settled into farming and domesticated animals, they stopped seeing themselves as equal to deer and trees. They invented gods as cosmic mediators to control rain, fertility, and plagues.
  • Why Polytheism Is Inherently Tolerant: If you believe in many gods, adding your neighbour's god to the pantheon is no threat. The Roman Empire happily absorbed Greek, Egyptian, and Syrian gods; it only persecuted Christians because Christians refused to show basic civic respect to the emperor's patron gods (seen as political treason, not heresy).
  • Monotheism's Trade-Off (Power vs. Intolerance): Claiming there is only one true God makes a religion fiercely missionary and unifying, yet far more prone to religious wars and persecutions against heretics.
  • The Problem of Evil & Dualism: If one God is all-powerful and all-good, why does evil exist? Dualism (like Zoroastrianism) solves this neatly with two cosmic forces (Good vs. Evil), but fails to explain what law governs the battle between them. In practice, mainstream monotheism quietly smuggled in dualism (the Devil / Satan) and polytheism (saints, angels, patron spirits) through the back door.
  • Natural-Law Religions (Buddhism, Jainism, Taoism): Religions without a creator god, founded on an impersonal cosmic law. In Buddhism, the root of suffering is not misfortune itself, but craving (tanha): our mind's constant demand to hold onto pleasant feelings and push away unpleasant ones. Liberation (nirvana) comes from observing reality as it is without craving.
  • Modern Humanist Religions (Worship of Homo Sapiens): The modern age is not "godless"; it merely replaced gods with humanism (and other natural-law ideologies like communism and capitalism). Humanism split into three rival sects:
    1. Liberal Humanism: Every individual human is sacred (human rights, democracy, individual freedom).
    2. Socialist Humanism: Humanity as a collective is sacred (equality over individual privilege).
    3. Evolutionary Humanism (e.g., Nazism): Homo sapiens is a mutable species that must be biologically upgraded to Übermensch or risk degeneration.

Mind-Blowing Anecdotes & Stories¶

  • The St Bartholomew's Day Massacre vs. 300 Years of Roman Persecution: Over three entire centuries of pagan Roman rule, polytheistic emperors executed at most a few thousand Christians. By contrast, over the next 1,500 years, Christians slaughtered millions of other Christians over tiny theological differences. On a single day in 1572 (St Bartholomew's Day), French Catholics killed between 5,000 and 10,000 French Protestants in 24 hours, more Christians killed in one day than the Roman Empire killed in 300 years.
  • Football vs. Communism: Why isn't football a religion? Because everyone knows humans invented the offside rule and FIFA can change it tomorrow. Why is Communism a religion under Harari's definition? Because Marxists believed the laws of history were a superhuman natural law discovered by Marx (complete with holy scriptures like Das Kapital, prophets, heretics like Trotsky, and a promised paradise).

Memorable Quotes¶

"Religion can thus be defined as a system of human norms and values that is founded on a belief in a superhuman order."

"Suffering arises from craving; the only way to be fully liberated from suffering is to be fully liberated from craving."

"In the 24 hours of St Bartholomew's Day, Christians killed more Christians than the polytheistic Roman Empire did in its entire existence."

Chapter 13: The Secret of Success¶

The Big Idea: History is a "level two" chaotic system that cannot be predicted; knowing how things happened does not mean they had to happen, and history does not evolve for the benefit of human happiness.

Core Concepts & Notes¶

  • The Hindsight Fallacy: Looking back, every major event (the rise of Christianity, the fall of Rome, the triumph of capitalism) looks inevitable. In reality, history is full of crossroads where tiny accidents flipped the coin. The people who know a period best (contemporaries) are almost always blind to what is about to happen.
  • Level One vs. Level Two Chaos:
    • Level One Chaos (like weather) does not react to predictions about it.
    • Level Two Chaos (like markets, politics, and history) reacts to predictions about itself, which makes exact prediction impossible. If a computer accurately predicted tomorrow's oil price, traders would act on the prediction today and immediately change tomorrow's price.
  • Why Study History Then? Not to predict the future, but to free ourselves from the past: to realise that our current social, political, and economic arrangements are neither natural nor inevitable, and therefore we have far more possibilities than we imagine.
  • History Is Blind to Human Well-Being: Cultures are like mental parasites (memes). A culture succeeds if it spreads and replicates well, not if it makes its human hosts happier or healthier (just like a virus).

Mind-Blowing Anecdotes & Stories¶

  • Emperor Constantine's Coin Flip (312 CE): In 306 CE, Christianity was still an obscure, persecuted Eastern mystery sect. A few years later, Emperor Constantine converted (likely for political unification reasons) and made it the empire's favoured faith. Had Constantine chosen Mithraism, Manichaeism, or stuck with paganism, the entire history of Europe and the world would look unrecognisable.
  • Karl Marx's Self-Defeating Prediction: Marx predicted a violent proletarian revolution in the most industrialised capitalist nations (like Britain and Germany). Precisely because capitalists read Marx and took his prediction seriously, they improved wages, allowed unions, and built welfare states, preventing the revolution in the West while it erupted instead in agrarian Russia.

Memorable Quotes¶

"History cannot be explained deterministically and it cannot be predicted because it is chaotic."

"We study history not to know the future, but to widen our horizons, to understand that our present situation is neither natural nor inevitable, and that we consequently have many more possibilities before us than we imagine."

"There is no proof that history is working for the benefit of humans, because we lack an objective scale on which to measure such benefit."
